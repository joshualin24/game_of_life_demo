\begin{abstract}
\noindent
We summarize a project studying how CNN-Transformer hybrid models learn
the one-step transition rule of Conway's Game of Life (GoL) on a
$40\times40$ toroidal grid. Across ten model versions we identify and fix
two distinct failure modes --- exposure bias under autoregressive rollout,
and a data-scaling regression hidden from aggregate validation metrics ---
and arrive at a $1.5$M-parameter model with zero errors on a 2{,}500-grid
stress test. We independently verify a recent claim that polynomial
activations have a strong inductive-bias advantage over ReLU for learning
GoL from very few parameters, and we present an embedding-space experiment
showing that sinusoidal positional encodings are empirically closer to
translation-equivariant than learned ones. Full details, all code, and a
20-page extended writeup are available in the project repository.
\end{abstract}

\section{Problem and Architecture}

Conway's Game of Life is a deterministic cellular automaton: each cell's
next state depends only on its own state and the count of live cells among
its 8 neighbors (B3/S23 rule). We treat one GoL step as a supervised
image-to-image translation problem on a $40\times40$ periodic (toroidal)
grid, and ask a neural network to predict the next grid state from the
current one.

Our core architecture (\emph{CNNTransformerV4}, used from Task~12 onward)
is a CNN-Transformer hybrid: two circular-padded convolutional layers
extract local features respecting the torus topology, which are then cut
into non-overlapping $4\times4$ patches, linearly projected, combined with
a \emph{learned} absolute positional embedding, passed through a
Transformer encoder to mix long-range information, and finally projected
back to per-cell logits. The convolutional stage guarantees
translation-equivariant local feature extraction by construction (weight
sharing plus circular padding); the Transformer stage adds global context
that a purely convolutional model of small receptive field cannot, at the
cost of the clean equivariance guarantee (Section~\ref{sec:embedding}).

\section{Training Evolution: Two Failure Modes and Their Fixes}
\label{sec:evolution}

\paragraph{Exposure bias (V5 $\to$ V7).} A model trained with pure
single-step teacher forcing (V5, val.\ loss $0.201$, precision/recall
$>99\%$) looks excellent on held-out single steps, but collapses when run
autoregressively: small errors compound because the model never sees its
own (imperfect) outputs during training. We first tried a
straight-through-estimator (STE) multi-step scheme (V6) to expose the
model to its own rollout during training with a differentiable path; this
\emph{diverges} after epoch~5 (val.\ loss rises from $0.234$ to $>1.0$),
which we trace to a systematic gradient bias introduced by the STE's hard
threshold. Replacing STE with \emph{scheduled sampling} (V7) --- gradually
increasing the probability of feeding the model its own previous
prediction instead of the ground truth --- fixes this cleanly: val.\ loss
$0.201$, precision $99.7\%$, recall $100\%$, stable across training.

\paragraph{A hidden data-scaling regression (V8 $\to$ V10).} Upsampling
high-density configurations $3\times$ (V8) improves per-grid accuracy
further (100\% precision, 291K params). Naively scaling the \emph{training
set} $5\times$ (V9) leaves aggregate validation metrics statistically
unchanged (val.\ loss $0.201$, precision $99.7\%$) --- but a
density-stratified failure search (500 random grids at each of five
densities, same seed across models) reveals that V9's per-grid error count
at density $0.80$ is nearly \emph{seven times worse} than V8's
(22{,}190 vs.\ 3{,}358 summed false positives/negatives). This regression
is invisible in aggregate metrics and only surfaces under targeted
subgroup auditing. Instead of more data, we scale the \emph{model}: V10
(128-dim, 6-layer Transformer, $1.5$M params, $5.15\times$ V8's size)
trained from scratch achieves \textbf{zero errors} across all 2{,}500 test
grids at every density (Figure~\ref{fig:v10}). The practical lesson: once a
model saturates its capacity, more data can silently trade average-case
performance for worse tail-case performance, and only per-subgroup
error auditing (not aggregate validation loss) can detect it.

\section{Embedding Geometry: Sinusoidal vs.\ Learned Positional Encoding}
\label{sec:embedding}

A CNN's feature map is a \emph{physical} embedding: because convolution is
translation-equivariant by construction, shifting the input shifts the
feature map by exactly the same amount, with the same content vector at
each location. A Transformer's patch embeddings, after a learned absolute
positional embedding is added, have no such architectural guarantee ---
each position slot is an independently learned vector with no structural
relationship to its neighbors.

We tested whether this shows up empirically by comparing two of our
trained models that differ (among other things) in positional encoding:
\emph{V2} uses a fixed 2D sinusoidal positional encoding (whose
angle-addition identities give it a closed-form near-equivariant
structure under translation), while \emph{V8} uses a learned absolute
positional embedding. We measured cosine similarity between patch
embeddings of a grid and of the same grid shifted by several offsets, for
both models, across glider and random configurations
(Figure~\ref{fig:v2v8}). V2's post-Transformer embeddings stay far more
similar under translation (mean cosine similarity $0.997$) than V8's
($0.890$), consistent with the theoretical prediction that sinusoidal
encodings impose a more equivariant geometry on the representation space.
(We note an acknowledged confound: V2 uses zero-padding in its
convolutional stage versus V8's circular padding, so this is not a
perfectly isolated ablation of positional encoding alone --- see the
extended writeup for a proposed fully controlled follow-up.)

\section{Independent Verification: Polynomial Activations for GoL}
\label{sec:verification}

Motivated by a recent paper (Ahmed \& Davis, 2026, arXiv:2606.23587)
claiming that a learnable second-degree polynomial activation
$\phi(x) = w_0 + w_1 x + w_2 x^2$ lets a minimal CNN learn GoL reliably
where ReLU fails almost completely, we independently reproduced this claim
from scratch rather than taking it on faith. We built two CNNs matching
the paper's minimal $L(1,1)$ architecture (two $1{\times}1$ convolutions
plus one $3{\times}3$ circular-padded convolution) that are
\textbf{identical except for the activation function}, exactly matching
the paper's reported parameter counts (ReLU: 25 params, polynomial: 34
params). Trained single-step, teacher-forcing, on a small dataset (100
train / 20 val.\ grids) over 10 random seeds each:

\begin{itemize}
\item \textbf{ReLU}: $1/10$ seeds converge to perfect validation accuracy; mean final accuracy $89.4\%$.
\item \textbf{Polynomial}: $10/10$ seeds converge; mean final accuracy $100.0\%$.
\end{itemize}

This confirms the paper's central claim on our own toroidal GoL setup
(Figure~\ref{fig:polyverify}). We then attempted to transfer this
activation into our larger multi-step CNN-Transformer model (V11, same
size as V8) by replacing GELU with polynomial activations in the CNN
stage. This \textbf{failed}: training diverged sharply after epoch~5 under
the scheduled-sampling regime, likely because the unbounded $w_2 x^2$ term
compounds through the autoregressive feedback loop in a way it cannot in
the paper's single-step setting. We report this as a negative result;
candidate fixes (a bounded/saturating polynomial variant, a separate
learning rate for polynomial coefficients, verifying single-step stability
before adding rollout) were identified but not yet pursued.

\section{Conclusion}

Three practical lessons emerge from this project. First, autoregressive
exposure bias in sequence/rollout models is best fixed with scheduled
sampling rather than a hard differentiable relaxation (STE), which can
introduce its own destabilizing gradient bias. Second, aggregate
validation metrics can hide severe, density-specific regressions; per-
subgroup error auditing is necessary once a model is scaled with more
data. Third, an activation function's inductive-bias advantage
demonstrated at minimal scale and single-step training does not
automatically transfer to a larger, multi-step, autoregressively-trained
model --- the interaction between an activation's stability properties
and the training regime matters as much as the activation itself.

Full code, all intermediate model checkpoints, the complete V1--V11
version history, additional embedding-space visualizations (rotation,
reflection, cell-perturbation transforms), and a fully detailed 20-page
writeup are available in the project repository
(\texttt{game\_of\_life\_demo/}).
